The New York residency audit mechanics most buyers underestimate

New York State is one of the most aggressive residency auditors in the country, and the audit risk is materially higher than California's parallel process. Two primary tests apply.

  • The domicile test. Where is your true, fixed, permanent home? NYS looks at family location, pet location, where your most valuable personal items are stored, where you receive medical care, where you worship, where you spend holidays, and where your professional and personal relationships are centered. All of these need to clearly point to Texas, not New York.
  • The 183-day statutory residency test. If you own or maintain a permanent place of abode in New York and spend more than 183 days of the year physically present in New York, you remain a New York resident for tax purposes regardless of your domicile. "Permanent place of abode" is interpreted broadly and includes rentals, pied-a-terres, and sometimes properties of family members.

What that means in practice. Keeping a Manhattan or Brooklyn apartment as a second residence after the move materially increases NYS audit risk and often costs the taxpayer the residency termination they thought they had completed. NYS has pursued and won cases years after the taxpayer believed the move was final. The specific audit evidence NYS examines is granular: cell phone location records, EZ-Pass toll records, credit card transaction locations, social media check-ins, and personal calendar data have all been used.

The single most expensive New York residency mistake is keeping a New York apartment "for when I come back to visit." Sell the New York home, close the equity, and establish a clean New York residency termination. That is the path.

Clean NY residency termination comes down to building the strongest possible evidence package for the inevitable audit review. The specific items that carry the most weight with NYS.

  1. Close out the NY home entirely. Selling the property is the single strongest domicile-change signal. A kept pied-a-terre, a sublet arrangement, or a rental you keep paying for all weaken the case meaningfully.
  2. Replace your identification stack in Texas quickly. New Texas driver's license, Travis County voter registration, Texas vehicle registration if you own a car, all handled in the opening weeks of the move.
  3. Pull your financial infrastructure to Texas. Primary banking, brokerage, retirement accounts, CPA, estate attorney, financial advisor, and any professional service relationships either move to Texas-based providers or transfer to national firms with your address updated to the Texas home.
  4. Bring the household entirely. Spouse, children, pets, meaningful personal property. A spouse who keeps commuting to NY while you claim Austin residency is one of the fastest ways to lose an audit.
  5. Minimize New York time. Target under 30 days of physical presence in NY per tax year, with 45 as a hard ceiling. Document the days you are not there with equal rigor to the days you are.
  6. Update everything institutional. Employer HR records, professional licensing boards, auto and health insurance, children's school enrollment, medical provider changes, and gym or club memberships all moved to Texas.
  7. File correctly for the transition year. NY Form IT-203 as a part-year resident, with the termination date documented, supported by closing-sale paperwork, Texas residency establishment paperwork, and physical-presence records.
  8. Keep contemporaneous records for three audit cycles. Travel receipts, EZ-Pass statements, credit card statements, cell phone location data, and calendar records all become relevant if NYS looks at your departure year or any subsequent year.

Timing the New York sale

The sequence I run with almost every NY-to-Austin client I represent: the NY unit goes up for sale, lands under contract, closes, and then the Austin purchase moves inside the following month. Multiple reasons this is the preferred structure.

  • Frees equity for a cash or near-cash Austin purchase, which produces stronger negotiating position on the Austin side.
  • Avoids dual-mortgage exposure during the move window.
  • Produces a cleaner NY residency termination (selling the NY property is the single strongest evidence of domicile change).
  • Simplifies the tax picture for the year of the move.

The main exception is the specific NY-to-Austin buyer who has clear strategic reasons to keep the NY property and the resources to accept the residency audit risk that creates. That is a tax-attorney conversation, not a realtor conversation.
