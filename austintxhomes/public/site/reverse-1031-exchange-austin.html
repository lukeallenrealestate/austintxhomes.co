Reverse 1031 Exchange Austin: When To Use It, When Not To

The decision point

Buy first, or sell first.

Every 1031 begins with a sequencing question. Will the investor close on the sale of the relinquished property before buying the replacement (the standard forward exchange), or will the replacement purchase happen first with the sale following (the reverse)?

Most investors prefer the forward exchange because it is cheaper, simpler, and the IRS safe harbor is well-trodden. The reverse exchange exists for situations where market dynamics force the sequence to flip. The decision tree below is how Luke walks Austin investors through the choice. For the standard delayed playbook, see the Austin 1031 main guide.

Branch one

If you can sell first, do.

If the relinquished property is already listed, under contract, or likely to sell quickly, a forward exchange is the right tool. Cost: typically $800 to $2,000 in QI fees. Complexity: moderate. Timeline: 45 days to identify, 180 days to close, from the sale.

Forward exchange is the correct answer when any of the following are true: the sold property is in a seller's market with low DOM; the investor can carry the identification risk of not knowing exactly which Austin property will be available in 45 days; the replacement property pool is deep enough that identification on day 45 is realistic. See the rules and deadlines reference for day-by-day mechanics.

Branch two

If you cannot sell first, reverse 1031 is the tool.

A reverse 1031 is used when the replacement property shows up before the relinquished property sells, and losing the replacement would be worse than the added cost of the reverse structure. Cost: typically $7,500 to $20,000 in QI and Exchange Accommodation Titleholder fees. Complexity: high. Timeline: 45 days to identify the property being sold, 180 days to close that sale, both from the replacement purchase date.

The reverse is correct when any of these conditions hold: an off-market or deeply underpriced Austin replacement will not wait for the sold property's closing; the relinquished property is likely to take longer than 180 days to sell; the investor has enough balance sheet to float both properties simultaneously during the reverse window.

Reverse exchanges in Austin most often get used on single-asset luxury or commercial deals in the $2M to $10M range where the replacement opportunity is rare enough to justify the extra structure cost.

Branch three

If you need improvements before occupancy, consider an improvement exchange.

A third variant, the Improvement Exchange (also called Construction Exchange or Build-to-Suit Exchange), allows the exchanger to use exchange proceeds to fund improvements to the replacement property during the 180-day window. The improvements become part of the replacement value calculation. This structure is used when the replacement property as-is would not satisfy the equal-or-greater-value rule, but the property plus planned improvements would.

Improvement exchanges can combine with reverse structures in a single transaction. Cost: $10,000 to $30,000 in combined fees. Complexity: highest. Used in Austin most often for developer 1031 buyers who are acquiring a value-add asset mid-renovation.

The EAT, plainly

The EAT structure in plain English.

The Exchange Accommodation Titleholder is a special-purpose entity that takes legal title to either the replacement property (in a reverse) or the improvements-in-progress (in an improvement exchange) so the exchanger does not take constructive receipt of both properties at the same time, which would disqualify the 1031.

The EAT is typically set up by the Qualified Intermediary as a single-purpose LLC owned by the QI's parent. The EAT holds title for up to 180 days under IRS Revenue Procedure 2000-37 safe harbor. During that window, the exchanger controls the property through a management agreement with the EAT and funds carrying costs. At the end of the window, title transfers from the EAT to the exchanger via deed, completing the exchange.

If the 180-day window expires before the relinquished property sells (in a reverse) or the improvements complete (in an improvement exchange), the safe harbor collapses and the entire transaction becomes taxable. For QI selection see the QI scorecard.

Decide the sequence first.

Decide the sequence first. Price the structure second. Call the QI third. If the Austin replacement that would otherwise get away is worth the five-figure cost delta over a forward exchange, the reverse is the right tool and the structure works. If the sold property can realistically fund inside the next six months, the forward exchange wins on cost and simplicity.
