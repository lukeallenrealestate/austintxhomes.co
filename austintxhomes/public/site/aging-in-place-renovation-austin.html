The core decision

Renovate the current home, or buy an aging-in-place-ready one

Both options can work. The right answer depends on current home features, equity position, projected residency timeline, and specific accessibility needs.

Option A

Renovate the current Austin home

Make the specific accessibility modifications needed to extend residency in the current home. Preserves established 65-plus homestead tax ceiling, familiar neighborhood, existing social connections, and avoids transaction costs on a move. Works best for homes with good aging-in-place bones (single-story or feasible first-floor primary conversion).

Best fit when:Current home is single-story or has feasible primary-on-first-floor; substantial equity in current home; long projected residency; attached to the neighborhood; established tax ceiling.
Option B

Buy an aging-in-place-ready home

Purchase a home that was either built or previously renovated to accessibility standards. Avoids the construction disruption of renovating the current home. Produces a cleaner long-term setup if the current home is a two-story with no feasible first-floor primary layout. Can combine with 55-plus community acquisition for additional amenity benefit.

Best fit when:Current home is two-story with no feasible retrofit; renovation cost would exceed move cost; specific 55-plus community desired; downsizing produces meaningful cash plus lower carrying cost.
The room-by-room accessibility checklist

What aging-in-place-ready actually means

Six zones in a typical Austin home where accessibility design matters most. Use these checklists either for renovation planning or for pre-purchase evaluation of candidate homes.

I

Entry

  • Zero-threshold or minimal-threshold front door
  • Ramp or no-step entry path (not just stairs)
  • 36-inch minimum entry doorway
  • Covered entry for weather protection during fumbling with keys
  • Motion-activated lighting on approach
  • Lever-style door handle, not round knob
II

Primary bedroom

  • First-floor location (not upstairs)
  • 36-inch minimum doorway
  • Walk-in closet with wide-open layout, not deep narrow
  • Open floor space of 60-inch diameter for turning radius
  • Light switches reachable from bed if needed
  • Clear floor path to primary bathroom without furniture obstacles
III

Primary bathroom

  • Zero-threshold or minimal-threshold shower
  • Shower bench or seat
  • Grab bars at toilet, shower, and bathtub (or grab-bar blocking for future install)
  • Comfort-height toilet (17-19 inches vs standard 14-15)
  • Lever-style faucets
  • 60-inch minimum turning radius
  • Non-slip flooring throughout
IV

Kitchen

  • Lever-style faucets
  • Pull-out shelves in base cabinets
  • Microwave at counter height, not above range
  • Front-control range or cooktop (safer than reach-over knobs)
  • Varied counter heights accommodating standing or seated use
  • Open floor space of 60-inch turning radius
  • Task lighting at work zones
V

Hallways + doorways

  • 36-inch minimum doorway widths throughout
  • 42-inch hallway widths where possible
  • Flush thresholds between rooms (no raised strips)
  • Lever-style door handles throughout
  • Night-light outlets at ankle height on key paths
  • Grab bar at any step where elevation changes
VI

Whole-home systems

  • Smart home automation for lights, locks, thermostat
  • Fall-detection and emergency alert system
  • Properly sized HVAC (overheating risk for seniors)
  • Water heater at accessible height for pilot or servicing
  • Electrical outlets at 18-inch height, switches at 42-inch
  • Smoke and CO detectors with hearing-impaired options if applicable

Where to start the Austin aging-in-place process

The high-leverage sequence runs in four steps. First: honest assessment of the current home against the six-zone checklist. Which zones already meet accessibility standards and which do not? Which non-meeting zones can be renovated economically and which cannot? Second: projection of specific functional needs over the next 10-20 years. Current mobility, projected mobility changes, caregiver access requirements if applicable, and family support geography. Third: renovate-vs-move financial analysis including current home equity, renovation cost projection, downsize alternative costs, and 65-plus tax ceiling implications. Fourth: specific decision on path forward (renovate current, buy aging-in-place-ready, or move to 55-plus community) with coordinated execution.

Aging-in-place FAQ

Common aging-in-place questions

What is CAPS and why does it matter for Austin aging-in-place renovation?

Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist. Designation from the National Association of Home Builders in partnership with AARP. CAPS-trained contractors understand specific accessibility standards (36-inch doorways, 60-inch turning radius, zero-threshold shower design, grab-bar blocking). Useful filter when selecting a contractor for aging-in-place renovation.

What are the most important aging-in-place modifications for an Austin home?

Four priorities: first-floor primary bedroom and bathroom; zero-threshold shower with proper slope drainage; 36-inch minimum doorways and 60-inch bathroom turning radius; grab-bar blocking in all bathrooms during renovation (standard practice adds plywood behind walls at bath, toilet, shower).

How much does aging-in-place renovation cost in Austin?

Minor (grab bars, lever handles, lighting): $2K-$8K. Mid-scope (zero-threshold shower, widened doorways, stair-lift): $15K-$40K. Major (first-floor primary addition, elevator, full retrofit): $80K-$250K+. Depends on home size and renovation extent.

Should I renovate my current Austin home or buy an aging-in-place-ready home?

Depends on: current home features (single-story often economic to renovate, two-story often better to move); current home equity and ongoing property tax position (65-plus ceiling benefits often favor staying); projected residency timeline and functional complexity.
