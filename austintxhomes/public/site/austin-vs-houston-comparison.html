The single biggest differentiator this page covers

Houston has had three separate billion-dollar flood events in the last decade. Austin has not.

Hurricane Harvey
60-plus inches of rain. Over 150,000 Harris County homes flooded. $125B total damage.

Why this matters for a buyer: Houston sits at 50 feet elevation on a flat coastal plain where heavy rain pools rather than runs off. Austin sits at 489 feet elevation with Hill Country terrain that drains east. Flood risk in Austin is concentrated to specific creek corridors a buyer can identify on the FEMA map and avoid. Flood risk in Houston is a metro-wide carrying-cost question that affects insurance, resale, and long-term property values. For many buyers, this one dimension is the entire decision.

Why flood risk gets its own section instead of a scorecard row

Most Austin vs Houston comparison content lists flood risk as a line item alongside weather or geography. That understates it. For a buyer who is not already living in a hurricane-zone metro, the carrying-cost and resale implications of Houston flood exposure deserve to be treated as a decision-defining variable rather than a bullet point.

The concrete numbers: Houston saw Memorial Day Flood 2015 ($500M-plus in damage), Tax Day Flood 2016 ($2B-plus), and Hurricane Harvey 2017 ($125B total damage, 150,000-plus flooded homes). These were not freak events in isolation. They were three separate billion-dollar flood events in a 25-month window. Hurricane Beryl in 2024 added additional metro-wide wind and flood damage. Harris County's elevation and flat coastal-plain drainage mean that heavy rain accumulates rather than runs off, which produces metro-wide flood exposure that specific property-level mitigation does not fully address.

Austin's flood risk is categorically different. Austin sits at 489 feet elevation with Hill Country topography that drains eastward through specific creek corridors (Shoal Creek, Walnut Creek, Barton Creek, Onion Creek). FEMA flood maps for Austin are high-resolution and most of the metro area sits well outside the 100-year and 500-year flood zones. A buyer can look at a specific property, check the FEMA map, and know with high confidence whether that address has flood exposure. In Houston, flood exposure is a metro-level question that affects insurance costs, resale, and insurability in ways that specific-property due diligence does not fully resolve.

The honest version: for a buyer who has never lived in a hurricane-zone metro, Houston flood risk is the single biggest wedge in this comparison.

The housing cost math, with real numbers

In 2026, the Houston metropolitan median single-family price prints near $340,000. The Austin equivalent prints near $545,000. For a 2,200-square-foot suburban family home in similar school zones, expect Houston to come in roughly $150,000 to $220,000 under the Austin ticket. The gap is larger than what you see on the Dallas-vs-Austin page, and it is the single most common reason a buyer skips Austin for Houston despite accepting the climate and flood exposure that comes with that choice.

The gap compresses at higher price bands. River Oaks, Memorial, and West University luxury pricing in Houston can match $3M-plus pricing in Westlake and Tarrytown. For buyers at $400,000 to $1,200,000, Houston is dramatically cheaper per square foot and per bedroom. Above $3,000,000 the comparison tightens but Houston still typically offers more house per dollar.

Carrying costs partially offset the Houston purchase advantage. Flood insurance for a Houston property in or near a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area typically runs $2,000 to $6,000 per year, and for properties in the 100-year zone it can run $4,000 to $10,000 per year. Homeowners insurance base rates in Houston also run higher than Austin due to wind and hail exposure. Over a 10-year hold period, these carrying costs can erode a meaningful portion of the Houston purchase savings. Property taxes are approximately comparable (Harris County effective rates run slightly higher than Travis County on average, but the gap is small).

The career industry axis that drives most decisions

If you take the Austin vs Houston decision down to the single variable that most often ends up driving the right answer, it is industry. Houston is the undisputed US capital of oil and gas, with ExxonMobil, Chevron, ConocoPhillips, Shell USA, Phillips 66, Occidental, and dozens of mid-sized operators plus the massive services and refining ecosystem. For a petroleum engineer, oil-and-gas finance professional, pipeline executive, or energy-services supply chain manager, Houston is not a lateral move from most other cities. It is where the industry's center of gravity sits.

Houston is also the global capital of concentrated medical care. Texas Medical Center is the largest medical complex in the world: 60 institutions, 10 million patient visits per year, MD Anderson Cancer Center (consistently ranked #1 US cancer hospital), Houston Methodist (nationally ranked across multiple specialties), Texas Children's Hospital (one of the top pediatric hospitals in the US), Baylor College of Medicine. For a nurse, physician, medical researcher, or healthcare administrator, Houston offers career depth that Austin does not match.

The Austin tech story is a stack rather than a single anchor: Tesla assembling vehicles in southeast Austin, the Parmer campus Apple finished at a billion dollars, Google downtown at 500 West Second, Oracle's $1.2 billion move out of Redwood City to Lakeshore in 2020, Samsung's $17 billion fab taking shape in Taylor, plus Meta, AMD, CrowdStrike, and Indeed. Behind the names sits a venture ecosystem that moves early-stage capital at a density Houston does not replicate. If the household's income earner is a technical IC, a product leader, a researcher, or a founder, that density is the whole case.

For most buyers, this one axis makes the decision cleaner than the full scorecard suggests.

Daily life, humidity, and the climate difference

Both metros have hot summers. The practical difference is humidity and duration. Houston's July average dew point sits at 73 Fahrenheit versus Austin's 68, which may read as a small gap on paper but is a meaningfully different sensory experience: Houston feels tropical during the full June-through-September window, with heat indices routinely exceeding ambient temperature by 10 to 15 degrees. Austin feels hot but drier, with Hill Country elevation and prevailing westerly breeze that moderates the humidity load.

Hurricane season is a factor Houston residents organize their year around (June through November, with peak activity August through October). Austin is far enough inland and elevated enough that hurricane impact is typically reduced to rain bands rather than direct wind-and-storm-surge threat. For a buyer accustomed to Northeast or West Coast climates with no annual hurricane planning, this is a tangible lifestyle difference.

Winter is milder in Houston than in Austin, which is a legitimate Houston advantage. Austin has produced a few hard freeze events in recent years (February 2021 produced metro-wide pipe failures and multi-day power outages). Houston freezes are rarer and less severe when they happen. For buyers prioritizing mild winters above all else, Houston wins that dimension.

Bottom line

The Austin vs Houston decision usually comes down to three questions in sequence: What industry is your household in. How much flood and hurricane risk are you willing to carry as a feature of daily life. Does the 35-40 percent housing cost advantage matter more to you than Hill Country lifestyle, lower humidity, and tech scene depth. For energy, healthcare, and pure-cost-driven buyers Houston usually wins. For tech, outdoor-oriented, and climate-sensitive buyers Austin wins.
